Morocco Travel Etiquette

  1. Home
  2. /
  3. Blog

  5. Morocco Travel Etiquette

Morocco is a welcoming, relatively easy country to travel in, and Moroccans are famously hospitable with visitors. A little cultural awareness goes a long way toward a smoother, more respectful trip — here's what's genuinely useful to know.

What to Wear

Morocco is a Muslim-majority country, and modest dress is appreciated, especially in medinas, smaller towns, and religious sites. Shoulders and knees covered is a safe general rule for both men and women in cities like Fes and Marrakech's old medina. That said, Morocco is diverse — you'll see everything from very traditional to very modern dress, and beach towns and resort areas are noticeably more relaxed. When in doubt, dress a little more conservatively than you would at home, and you'll blend in fine.

Greetings

A handshake is the standard greeting, and it's common (though not universal) for greetings between men and women to be more reserved — following the other person's lead is the easiest approach. Use your right hand for eating, giving, and receiving, as the left hand is traditionally considered less polite for these things. Always ask before photographing people directly, particularly in the souks — most people are happy to say yes if you ask first.

Mint Tea

Moroccan mint tea (atay) is a genuine expression of hospitality, not just a drink — you'll often be offered a glass the moment you sit down in a shop or someone's home. It's polite to accept at least a small glass, even if you don't finish it. Watching it poured from height into small glasses is part of the ritual, and it's usually quite sweet by default.

Bargaining in the Souks

Haggling is normal and expected in the souks — prices for handicrafts, textiles, and most non-food items are rarely fixed. It's treated as a friendly, expected part of the interaction rather than something adversarial; a good-natured back-and-forth is the norm, and walking away is a legitimate (and often effective) negotiating tactic. Fixed-price shops and modern stores are the exception, not the rule.

Fridays and Ramadan

Friday is the main day of communal prayer, and some shops and businesses in smaller towns close or reduce hours around midday. If you're traveling during Ramadan, many restaurants have reduced daytime hours, and it's respectful to be discreet about eating, drinking, or smoking in public during fasting hours — evenings, on the other hand, come alive with iftar meals and a noticeably festive atmosphere.
